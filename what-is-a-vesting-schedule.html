What Is a Vesting Schedule?

Learn what vesting means for employer retirement contributions and equity awards, plus terms to verify in plan documents.

The short answer

Vesting describes when an employee gains ownership of an employer-provided benefit, such as certain retirement contributions or equity awards. A plan can have immediate vesting, gradual vesting or a cliff schedule. The specific plan or award agreement controls the dates and conditions.

What to check

Vesting is different from being eligible to participate. You might be able to contribute to a plan now while employer contributions vest over time. Leaving a job can affect unvested amounts, but the result depends on the plan, termination date and governing documents.

Use the right record

Ask for the schedule in writing, determine which contributions are subject to it, and confirm how service time is counted. Do not assign a full cash value to an unvested award when comparing compensation.

Official reference

Investor.gov notes that employer-sponsored retirement plans can include employer matching contributions; plan terms govern participation and benefits. Read the source.

Cliff vs graded vesting: an illustrative equity grant

A cliff gives no vested portion until a specified point; graded vesting releases portions over time. For an illustrative 1,000-unit equity award, a four-year schedule might vest 250 units at the first-year cliff and the remaining 750 monthly over three years. That is one grant example, not a universal rule for equity or retirement plans.

Retirement contributions and equity do not share one set of rules

For a qualified retirement plan, your own contributions are fully vested; employer contributions can have different plan-defined rules. The IRS vesting guide explains that distinction. Equity grants use their own award conditions. Do not apply an equity cliff example to a 401(k) match.

If the benefit is an employer retirement contribution, first identify how a 401(k) match works, then check its vesting terms.

Employee contributions vs an unvested employer match

For retirement plans, the IRS explains that your own employee contributions are always fully vested. Employer contributions may follow a schedule under the plan; some arrangements require immediate vesting.

For a simplified example, assume the account contains $10,000 of employee contributions and $4,000 of employer contributions, with no investment changes. If the employer portion is 50% vested under the applicable plan, the vested amount is $10,000 + $2,000 = $12,000. Applying 50% to the entire $14,000 would incorrectly reduce the employee-owned portion.

Before changing jobs, confirm the service date used by the plan, the next vesting milestone and any special rules. Equity award schedules are separate; do not apply this retirement example to stock options or RSUs.

Vested does not necessarily mean sold or paid out

For stock options, vesting can make options exercisable but does not pay the exercise price or sell shares. For an RSU, check settlement conditions as well as vesting. After confirming the vested percentage, use the vested equity value calculator only for an assumed gross illustration, not after-tax sale proceeds.

For the award-type distinction, review employee stock options vs RSUs alongside the grant documents.

Frequently asked questions

What is cliff vesting?

Vesting that starts at a specified point rather than gradually before that point, according to the plan or award.

Are my own 401(k) contributions subject to the employer’s vesting schedule?

Your own contributions to a qualified retirement plan are fully vested; employer contributions can have different rules.

Does a vested option become cash automatically?

No. Exercising, paying the exercise price and selling shares are separate steps.

What should I check if I leave the employer?

Review vested and unvested portions, the service-counting method, forfeiture terms and any option exercise deadline.
