Credit card cycle changes

Why Did My Credit Card Billing Period Change?

A billing period can shift after an issuer-approved due-date or cycle change, but the exact reason and effective date depend on the account. Compare the dates on your newest statement and confirm any unexpected change with the issuer.

What belongs in one period

A billing period (also called a billing cycle) is the date range covered by one statement. It is not necessarily the same as a calendar month. Many card cycles run about 28 to 31 days, but the exact start and end dates depend on the account and issuer. Use the dates printed on your statement rather than assuming every cycle is 30 days.

Posted purchases, credits, payments, fees and interest are assigned according to the issuer’s posting and cycle rules. A pending purchase may not appear on that statement if it has not posted by the cutoff; it can show up in a later cycle. See how a payment posting date differs from its due date, and check your issuer’s card agreement for account-specific posting rules.

What is a 28-day billing cycle?

It is a statement period covering 28 days of account activity under the issuer's posting rules. For example, a statement covering February 1 through February 28, 2026 spans 28 days. The payment due date for that statement is a separate date printed on the bill.

A 28-day cycle does not mean every purchase must be paid exactly 28 days after you make it. A purchase posted near the start of the cycle and one posted near its end can appear on the same statement and share its payment deadline.

How to check a cycle that changed

  1. Compare the opening and closing dates on two consecutive statements.
  2. Check whether you requested a new due date or opened the account recently.
  3. Confirm the next payment deadline and any existing autopay schedule with the issuer.

Use the statement dates for the actual length. Capital One describes typical credit card cycles as 28 to 31 days; individual account terms control.

Closing date is not the due date

The closing date determines the statement snapshot. The due date tells you when the required payment must reach the issuer. The CFPB explains that issuers generally must adopt procedures to ensure periodic statements are mailed or delivered at least 21 days before the due date. See closing date vs. due date for the practical difference and timing example.

Why period length matters

When interest is already accruing daily, both the number of days and the balances during those days affect the charge. A flat APR ÷ 12 estimate can differ from an actual statement.

Illustration: 28 days compared with 31 days

Assume a constant $1,000 balance, 24% APR, a 365-day divisor, no grace period and no compounding, payments or new activity. Simple daily interest would be $1,000 × 0.24 ÷ 365 × 28 = about $18.41 for 28 days, versus about $20.38 for 31 days. The $1.97 difference comes only from the extra three days.

This is an arithmetic illustration, not a prediction of your bill: issuers can use different balance methods, rounding and contractual rules. Use the credit card interest calculator for estimates and the statement for the actual charge.

Transactions posted after closing generally belong to a later period even when they appear in the current balance. Compare statement balance vs. current balance. A billing cycle is also separate from the purchase grace period. For the payment conditions, read how to avoid credit card interest.

Can you change your billing cycle?

Some issuers let cardholders request a different payment due date, which may also affect the billing cycle. Approval, available dates and the effective cycle vary. Contact the issuer and confirm the transition date, minimum payment due during the change, and whether an existing statement deadline remains in effect. Until the issuer confirms the change, follow the due date on your current statement.

For the distinction between the statement cutoff and payment deadline, see credit card closing date vs. due date. If you are checking how long you have to pay, read how a credit card grace period works.

Check the actual account terms

Credit card rates, fees, posting rules and promotional terms can vary by issuer and transaction. Read your current statement and agreement for the account-specific answer. Federal rules address periodic statement timing; see Regulation Z, 12 CFR § 1026.5. For a plain-language overview of cycle dates and changing a due date, see the issuer’s credit card billing cycle guide.

Frequently asked questions

Is a billing period always 30 days?

No. Cycle length can vary by calendar and issuer.

Does a purchase on closing day appear?

It depends on when the transaction posts under issuer processing rules.

Does the due date end the billing period?

No. The cycle closes first, and the payment deadline comes later.

Can I change my billing cycle or payment due date?

Some issuers accept requests to change the due date or cycle. Contact your issuer, confirm the effective date, and keep following the current statement until the change is confirmed.

Does a 28-day billing cycle mean I have 28 days to pay?

No. The billing cycle describes the period covered by a statement. The payment deadline appears separately as the due date. Follow that date rather than counting 28 days from a purchase.
