Debt and credit planning

Debt Payoff Plan

A debt payoff plan organizes balances, rates, due dates and the amount you can apply each month.

The short answer

List every debt, confirm the statement balance and APR, choose a payoff method and keep required payments current while applying extra money.

Calculate the extra payment your budget can support

Start with take-home income and essential spending, not the amount a payoff calculator says would clear the debt fastest. For an illustrative month, $3,200 of take-home income minus $2,350 of living costs and a $200 buffer leaves $650 for debt payments. If required payments total $450, the extra amount is $200, not $650.

Extra debt payment = debt-payment budget − all required payments

Do not count a payment twice. A loan installment already included in living costs must be moved to the debt list before using this example. Include irregular bills and confirm that cash arrives before each due date. A monthly surplus can still hide a shortfall early in the month.

When the available amount is not enough

If the budget does not cover required payments, choosing snowball or avalanche will not solve that gap. Contact creditors before missing a payment to ask what assistance may be available, and consider asking a nonprofit credit counselor to review your budget and options. Avoid planning around an unconfirmed rate reduction.

Review the plan after each statement

Record the payment that posted, new interest, fees and the new balance. A lower balance is not always equal to the amount you paid. Use the updated minimums and available cash for the next month, keeping extra payments separate from new borrowing.

Build a debt payoff plan in four steps

  1. List the current balance, APR, required payment and due date for every account.
  2. Set aside enough to cover all required payments before allocating extra money.
  3. Choose one target balance using either the snowball or avalanche method.
  4. Update the list after each statement closes; a rate change, fee or promotional end date can change the plan.

Check actual progress against the payoff plan

At each statement, reconcile the opening balance, added interest or fees, new charges, payments and closing balance. A $150 payment does not always reduce the balance by $150.

For an example with no new purchases or fees, a $1,000 opening balance plus $20 of interest minus a $150 payment leaves $870. Principal fell by $130. If the statement differs from your forecast, check transaction and posting dates before increasing the next payment.

When an account reaches zero, confirm whether any remaining interest or fees are due, then update the next target and total budget. Keep the Debt Snowball Payment Calculator inputs current and compare them with the Debt Avalanche Method if rates change.
