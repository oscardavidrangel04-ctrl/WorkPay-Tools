Credit Freeze vs Fraud Alert: What Is the Difference?

A credit freeze restricts access to a credit file for many new-credit checks. A fraud alert tells creditors to verify identity before certain new-credit actions.

Credit freeze versus fraud alert

A freeze makes the file unavailable to most prospective creditors until you lift it. A fraud alert leaves the file accessible but requires identity-verification steps. Both are free under federal law; neither guarantees that all identity theft is prevented.

How to place each one

To freeze all three nationwide files, contact Equifax, Experian and TransUnion separately. For an initial fraud alert, contacting one nationwide bureau is enough because it must notify the other two.

How long they last

A freeze generally remains until you lift it. An initial fraud alert generally lasts one year; an extended alert has different eligibility and duration. Check current bureau instructions before applying for credit.

Which should you choose?

A freeze offers stronger control over new access to your file. An alert can be appropriate when you suspect fraud and want lenders to perform additional checks. You may use both if the situation warrants it.

Official reference

Check current guidance from Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. This page is educational and cannot determine an individual credit score or legal outcome.

Frequently asked questions

Is a credit freeze the same as a fraud alert?

No. A freeze restricts access for many new-credit checks; an alert requires additional identity verification.

Do I contact all three bureaus?

For a freeze at all three, yes. For an initial fraud alert, contact one and it must notify the others.

Does a credit freeze affect a credit score?

Placing or lifting a security freeze does not affect a credit score.
