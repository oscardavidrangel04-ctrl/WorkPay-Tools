Debt Avalanche Method

The debt avalanche method directs extra money to the balance with the highest APR while required payments continue on other debts.

The short answer

It is an interest-focused ordering method. Verify rates, promotional terms and required payments from account records.

Debt avalanche example: choose by APR, not balance

Assume three separate accounts: a $900 balance at 18% APR, a $3,000 balance at 27% APR, and a $5,000 balance at 9% APR. The avalanche target is the $3,000 account because its rate is highest. It is neither the smallest nor the largest debt.

If required payments are $35, $95 and $120, they total $250. With a $450 debt-payment budget, the extra $200 goes to the 27% account. Its planned payment is $295; the other two still receive $35 and $120. Those payments total $450. This is an allocation example, not a forecast of interest charges or the payoff date.

Check exceptions before using a simple APR order

Past-due amounts, essential secured obligations and promotional deadlines can require separate attention. An expiring deferred-interest offer is not necessarily the same as a normal 0% APR promotion. Check its payoff deadline and agreement before deciding where extra cash goes.

For several balances inside one credit card, the issuer's payment-allocation rules control where the money is applied. This example assumes separate accounts you can pay individually. Check the next statement before moving the full planned payment to another target.

How the debt avalanche sequence works

First, list the debts by APR from highest to lowest. Keep required payments current on every account. Apply the available extra amount to the highest-rate balance; when it is paid, move the same amount to the next highest APR.

This ordering can reduce interest when other facts are equal, but promotional rates and changing account terms need to be checked. Compare it with the debt snowball method.
