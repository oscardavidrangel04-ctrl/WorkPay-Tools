Salary Increase vs. Inflation

Learn the difference between a nominal pay raise and a real increase in purchasing power, with a simple comparison formula.

Reviewed October 17, 2026

Nominal raise and real purchasing power

A nominal raise is the percentage printed in a pay offer. A real raise adjusts that change for inflation.

Approximate real change = salary raise % − inflation %

For a more precise comparison: ((1 + raise rate) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1) × 100.

Example: 5% raise with 3% inflation

The quick estimate is about 2%. The compound formula gives approximately 1.94%. This does not mean every personal expense rose by exactly 3%; individual budgets can differ.

What else changes compensation?

Health benefits, employer retirement contributions, bonuses, paid time off and schedule changes can matter alongside salary. Use the Compensation Package Calculator for a broader comparison.

Use the same time window for pay and price changes

A raise effective this month is not automatically comparable with an inflation figure covering a different period. Note the dates for both numbers. For a year-over-year comparison, use the pay rate and price measure for aligned dates where possible.

The pay rate and earnings received are different measures

A $60,000 annual salary raised 6% becomes a $63,600 annual rate. If the increase applies for only half the year, simplified earnings for that year are $30,000 plus $31,800, or $61,800. That is 3% more than $60,000, even though the ongoing rate increased 6%.

State whether you are comparing annualized pay rates or actual earnings received. Then use the salary increase formula on that consistent basis. The inflation adjustment still does not calculate personal take-home pay.

Frequently asked questions

Does a raise above inflation guarantee more take-home pay?

Not necessarily. Taxes, deductions, benefits and personal expenses can change the result.

What is a nominal salary increase?

It is the stated percentage increase before adjusting for inflation.

Should I use actual annual earnings or my new salary rate?

Either can answer a useful question, but label it. The new rate describes ongoing pay; actual earnings account for how much of the year the rate applied.

Does national inflation describe my exact household cost increase?

No. Your mix of housing, transport, health and other expenses can differ from the price measure used. Treat the comparison as a benchmark.

How to estimate your real raise

A simple approximation subtracts inflation from the raise percentage. For example, a 6% salary increase with 3% inflation is roughly a 3% improvement. A more precise calculation is ((1 + raise rate) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1) × 100.
