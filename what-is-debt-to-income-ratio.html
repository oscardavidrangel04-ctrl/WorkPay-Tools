Debt and income

What Is Debt-to-Income Ratio?

Learn the basic debt-to-income ratio formula, why gross monthly income is commonly used, and what it does not measure.

The debt-to-income ratio formula

Debt-to-income ratio, or DTI, compares monthly debt payments with gross monthly income. A simple calculation is monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income, expressed as a percentage. Lenders can define the inputs differently, so a personal estimate is not a lending decision.

Debt-to-income ratio example: payments, not total balances

Suppose the monthly debt payments included in your calculation are $1,200 for a mortgage, $300 for a car loan and $100 in required card payments. The total is $1,600. With $5,000 of gross monthly income, DTI is $1,600 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 32%.

The example uses the monthly payments, not the total mortgage or card balance. It also uses income before payroll deductions. Specific lenders can include or calculate obligations differently, so confirm their rules before treating your estimate as an application result.

Why DTI does not replace a budget

Food, utilities, childcare and savings needs can still affect affordability even when they are not part of that particular debt ratio. Compare the payment total with take-home income separately when planning your cash flow. The CFPB explains the standard gross-income formula.

What DTI leaves out of a household budget

DTI does not show every part of a household budget. It may not capture variable spending, savings needs, income instability or all bills. A lower ratio is not a guarantee of approval, and a higher ratio does not by itself prove that a payment cannot be managed.

Choose consistent monthly income and payments

Use current statements to identify recurring debt payments and use a representative gross-income figure. Keep the calculation separate from net pay and from credit utilization; they answer different questions.

DTI and credit utilization can move in different directions

Debt-to-income ratio compares payments with income. Credit utilization compares reported revolving balances with limits. They do not use the same denominator and should not share one percentage label.

Compare the inputs, not just the percentages

For a simple illustration, $500 of included monthly debt payments divided by $5,000 of gross monthly income gives a 10% DTI. Separately, a $2,000 card balance divided by a $4,000 limit gives 50% utilization. Neither percentage replaces the other.

A pay raise can change DTI without changing a card balance or limit. A limit change can change utilization without changing gross income. For a lender application, ask which income and obligations count. For your own calculation, use the DTI calculator and keep a separate utilization calculation.

Debt-to-income ratio worksheet for a mortgage estimate

For a mortgage estimate, start with the proposed housing payment and other monthly obligations the lender requires. A principal-and-interest quote alone may omit housing costs such as property taxes, insurance or association dues. Ask which costs belong in the lender's calculation and avoid adding an escrowed cost twice.

Frequently asked questions

Is DTI calculated from take-home pay?

The common formula uses gross monthly income before deductions. Use the lender's specified definition for an application and take-home income separately for cash budgeting.

Does a low DTI guarantee loan approval?

No. A lender can consider other factors and apply its own eligibility rules. A calculator result is not an approval decision.

Should I use only the mortgage principal and interest for DTI?

Not necessarily. A mortgage assessment may include other housing obligations. Ask the lender for the complete monthly housing amount and avoid counting the same cost twice.
