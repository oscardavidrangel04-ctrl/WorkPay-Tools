Credit limits

Why Did My Credit Limit Decrease?

Your issuer may lower a credit limit after reviewing payment history, credit-report information, income or account use, or after changing its lending policies. The notice from your issuer is the place to find the reason for your account. A lower limit can also reduce available credit and raise utilization even when your balance stays the same.

Why credit limits can decrease

Issuers periodically review accounts and may change a limit based on their assessment of risk. Possible factors can include account activity, payment history, changes shown on a credit report, updated income information, card inactivity or an issuer-wide policy change. A reduction does not by itself prove that any one factor caused the decision.

The account notice is more useful than a general list: it may state a reason or explain how to request one. Check the effective date and whether the change applies to one card or several accounts.

Does lowering your credit limit affect your credit score?

In this example, the balance did not increase, but it uses a larger share of the available limit. That may affect the amount of credit available for future purchases and can change the utilization shown for the account. A lower limit does not automatically lower a credit score, but a higher utilization percentage can be one factor that scoring models consider. What to do after a credit limit decrease

  1. Read the notice, including its effective date and any stated reason or process for requesting more information.
  2. Check your current balance, available credit and any scheduled automatic payments or pending transactions.
  3. Review your statement and credit report for information that may be incorrect.
  4. Keep required payments current and contact the issuer for an account-specific explanation or to ask about its review process.

Do not assume a lower limit means the account is closed, the APR changed or an over-limit fee will apply. Those matters have separate terms and notices.

Issuer notice and account protections

The CFPB explains that card issuers generally can reduce a credit limit. In many situations an adverse-action notice must provide reasons for the action or explain how to request them. The CFPB also explains limits on certain over-limit fees or penalty pricing after a limit reduction. Read the CFPB guidance on credit-limit decreases and your own account terms for the details that apply.

Does your credit limit reset after a payment?

Your credit limit is the borrowing ceiling set by the issuer, not a monthly allowance that automatically resets. Paying down the balance can restore available credit; it does not by itself reverse a credit limit decrease.

Check which number changed

With a $1,000 limit and a $400 balance, available credit would be about $600 before pending charges or holds. A cleared $100 payment could raise it to $700 while the limit stays $1,000. If the issuer instead lowered the limit to $700, that is a separate account change.

Available credit may not update immediately after a payment. For example, Capital One explains payment holds; other issuers have their own policies. Check the payment status and ask the issuer about a delay rather than assuming it reduced your limit.

Can you get the old limit restored?

You can request a review, but the issuer does not have to restore the old limit. Make the request specific: identify the notice date, ask which reason led to the decision and explain any information you believe is outdated or wrong.

Prepare these details before calling

  • The old and new limits, current balance and effective date of the decrease.
  • The issuer's stated reason and any supporting record that contradicts it.
  • Current income information if the issuer asks for it; provide accurate figures through its secure channel.
  • A question about whether reconsideration requires a hard inquiry and when you can request another review.

If the decision relied on an inaccurate report entry, follow the credit-report error process as well as contacting the issuer. A report correction and a limit-restoration decision are separate steps; neither guarantees the other. The CFPB explains notices for credit-limit reductions.

Credit limit decrease FAQ

Why did my credit limit decrease?

The issuer may reduce a limit after an account review, changes in payment or credit history, account activity, or a broader risk decision. The issuer notice or a request for reasons may provide account-specific information.

Can a lower credit limit affect credit utilization?

Yes. If the balance stays the same while the credit limit falls, the balance represents a larger percentage of that limit.

What should I do after a credit limit decrease?

Read the issuer notice, check the effective date and available credit, review the account and credit report for errors, and contact the issuer for account-specific information.

Can I ask the issuer to restore my credit limit?

You can ask the issuer, but restoration is not guaranteed. The issuer can explain its current requirements and review process.
