Does a Balance Transfer Affect Your Credit?

A transfer can change where a balance is reported and, if you open a new card, can add an application inquiry. No exact score change can be predicted.

How a balance transfer changes credit utilization

Moving debt changes which card carries the balance. Check each card and the combined total rather than looking only at the card you paid off. FICO explains why reported debt and revolving utilization matter, but a ratio calculation does not predict a score change.

Worked example using two existing cards

Assume Card A already has a $5,000 limit and a $3,000 balance. Card B already has a $4,000 limit and no balance. Before a transfer, Card A uses 60% of its limit and the combined utilization is $3,000 ÷ $9,000 = 33.33%.

Move all $3,000 to Card B with a hypothetical 3% fee added to that card. After both issuers report the completed transfer, Card A has $0 and Card B has $3,090. Card B now uses 77.25% of its $4,000 limit, while the combined ratio is 34.33%. The old card shows less debt, but the fee increased total debt by $90.

This example assumes both cards were already open, unchanged limits and no other activity. A newly opened card changes the comparison because it adds a separate account and may add available credit. Use the credit utilization calculator to check a balance-to-limit percentage, not to forecast points.

Check reporting dates before comparing scores

The receiving card may report its new balance before the old card reports the payment. Compare each account's reported balance and update date with the issuer's records. Do not assume a temporary mismatch means you owe the transferred amount twice. If an amount remains wrong, use the credit-report error guide to organize the evidence.

Closing the old card is a separate decision. In the example, if Card A closes and its $5,000 limit no longer counts toward available revolving credit, $3,090 across the remaining $4,000 limit is 77.25%. Review how closing a credit card can affect credit before treating closure as a required part of the transfer.

A new account is a separate event

Applying for a new balance-transfer card may lead to a credit inquiry and a newly reported account. The guide to how long credit inquiries stay on a report separates the reporting timeline from possible scoring effects. A transfer offered on an existing account does not necessarily involve a new application.

Payments still matter

Keep making required payments on the old card until the transfer is complete. Check balance-transfer processing times and completion checks before assuming that a request has paid off the old balance. A processing delay does not erase the old account’s due date. After completion, verify the residual balance and pay the new account as required. Avoid promises that a transfer always helps or hurts a score.

Check the actual account terms

Credit card rates, fees, posting rules and promotional terms can vary by issuer and transaction. Read your current statement and agreement for the account-specific answer. Primary reference: consumer guidance from the CFPB or card issuer.

Frequently asked questions

Will a balance transfer automatically raise my score?

No. The result depends on reported balances, limits, inquiries and the scoring model.

Does a transfer close my old card?

Not necessarily. Check the issuer’s account status after the transfer.

Should I stop paying the old card immediately?

No. Continue required payments until the transfer is confirmed.
