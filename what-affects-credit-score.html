What Affects a Credit Score? Reported Account Factors

Credit scores use report information, but the result depends on the model, report data and timing.

The short answer

Credit scores are calculated from information in credit reports. Models may consider payment history, balances relative to limits, account age, recent applications and account types—but no single action predicts a specific point change across all scores.

If you are investigating a recent change, use the checklist in why a credit score may drop to organize the information to review.

What to review

FICO models commonly group report data into five categories. FICO publishes the percentages below for its general scoring framework; they are not a promise of how many points an individual action will change, and other score models can use different methods.

FICO categoryPublished general shareExamples of report information
Payment history35%On-time and late payments, severity and recency
Amounts owed30%Balances, available credit and utilization
Length of credit history15%Age of oldest and newest accounts and average history
New credit10%Recent applications and newly opened accounts
Credit mix10%Variety of credit accounts in the file

These published category shares are not a personal score calculator or fixed weights for every model. The importance of a factor depends on the complete credit file and scoring version. For balance-to-limit context, see credit utilization ratio. If report information is inaccurate, follow the process in credit report error.

FICO vs. VantageScore: why the model matters

FICO and VantageScore are different scoring systems, and each can have multiple versions. They may evaluate credit-report details differently and produce different results from the same file. Lenders may also request scores tailored to a product, use a particular bureau’s data, or consider additional application information separately. Ask a lender which score it uses if you need to understand a specific decision; there is no single score that every lender sees.

When the score is labeled with that version, refer to the VantageScore 4.0 guide rather than assuming all versions use identical methods.

Official reference

The CFPB overview of credit scores explains that scores are calculated from credit-report information and that models may consider payment history, debt, account age, credit use, applications and negative events. For the FICO framework, see myFICO’s factor explanation.

Frequently asked questions

Does one late payment affect every score the same way?

No. The report details, model and the rest of the file can change the effect.

Does income appear in a credit score?

Income is generally not part of a consumer credit score, although lenders can consider income separately.

Is keeping utilization below 30% required?

No single percentage guarantees a score or approval. Models and credit files differ; the CFPB says models may consider how close balances are to limits.

Account age and card decisions

Account history changes over time, while closing a card can alter available credit now. Read how credit age works, the card-closing guide and how long inquiries appear.

What does not affect a FICO Score directly?

FICO Scores do not use your salary, employer or occupation. Lenders may consider those details separately when deciding whether a payment is affordable. A raise can improve your household budget without directly adding points to a FICO Score.

Similarly, the APR on a card is not itself a FICO scoring input. The balance and payment history can matter, so paying more interest is not a way to buy a better score. Checking your own report also does not create the type of hard application inquiry that affects scores.

Read FICO's list of excluded information. Use the educational credit factor checklist to identify report items to inspect; it cannot predict a score or lending decision.
