Debt Snowball Method

The debt snowball method directs extra money to the smallest balance first while maintaining required payments on other debts.

The short answer

It can create a visible sequence of paid accounts, but it may not minimize interest. Compare the actual balances, rates and payment requirements.

Debt snowball example: roll the payment forward

Suppose you owe $400, $1,200 and $3,500 on three separate accounts. Their required payments are $25, $50 and $100. With a $300 monthly debt-payment budget, the $175 of required payments leaves $125 extra. The first target is the $400 balance, which receives $150 in total.

Once that account is fully paid, its $25 minimum and the $125 extra become available for the next target. If the remaining required payments have not changed, the $1,200 account receives $200 and the $3,500 account receives $100. Your total budget stays $300; you have not created extra income.

Do not assume a fixed number of payoff months

These figures show the payment order only. Interest, fees, new purchases and a changing minimum affect the time needed to clear each account. Confirm the final balance before treating an account as paid, and recheck the remaining statements each month.

The tradeoff is rate order: your smallest balance may not have the highest APR. Keep an eye on what the other accounts cost while you work on the first target. A plan you can maintain matters, but the snowball method is not a promise of the lowest total interest.

How the debt snowball sequence works

First, list the debts by current balance from smallest to largest. Keep required payments current on every account. Apply the available extra amount to the smallest balance; after it is paid, roll that same payment amount to the next balance.

This is different from the debt avalanche method, which orders balances by APR. Compare both methods in Debt Avalanche vs Debt Snowball.
