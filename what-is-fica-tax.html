What Is FICA Tax on a Paycheck?

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It funds Social Security and Medicare. Most covered employees pay 6.2% for Social Security plus 1.45% for Medicare, or 7.65% when both apply to the same wages. Federal income tax is separate; wage limits and Additional Medicare can change the calculation.

What does FICA stand for?

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It is the federal employment-tax framework that funds Social Security and Medicare. On a pay stub, it may appear as “FICA,” “Social Security,” “OASDI,” “Medicare,” or similar payroll labels.

Employers generally withhold the employee share from covered wages and also pay an employer share. The Social Security Administration explains that FICA and the self-employment counterpart, SECA, fund Social Security and Medicare programs.

What taxes are included in FICA for payroll withholding?

FICA includes Social Security and Medicare taxes. Federal income tax withholding is a separate deduction, not a third FICA component. Medicare is part of FICA, but Medicare alone is not the whole FICA deduction.

What does FICA fund?

They help fund these social insurance programs rather than a personal savings account. Amounts withheld are not a dollar-for-dollar deposit into your own future benefits. Payroll may list the components separately even when both are described as FICA.

The ordinary combined rate is 7.65% for the employee and 7.65% for the employer when the same wages are subject to both regular components. The employer’s share is not another 7.65% deduction from employee pay. These are employee payroll rules, not a self-employment tax calculation.

Is FICA a federal tax or federal income tax?

Yes, FICA is a federal payroll tax. No, it is not the same as federal income tax. FICA covers Social Security and Medicare. Keep those amounts separate from income-tax withholding when reading a paycheck or using a tax estimator. A pay stub label such as “Federal,” “FIT” or “Federal withholding” usually refers to income-tax withholding. Confirm your payroll provider’s labels.

Are FICA wages the same as gross pay?

Not always. Gross pay is what you earn before deductions; FICA wages are the earnings subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes. Some benefits can change those taxable wages. A traditional employee 401(k) contribution generally reduces federal income-tax wages but remains subject to FICA.

On Form W-2, Box 3 reports Social Security wages and Box 5 reports Medicare wages. Once earnings pass the Social Security wage base, those boxes can differ. Compare W-2 Box 1 vs Box 5 for the distinction between income-tax and Medicare wages.

What is FICA EE on my paycheck?

Look for Social Security and Medicare withholding, which may have separate lines. “SS” or “OASDI” commonly means Social Security; “MED” or “HI” commonly means Medicare. “EE” commonly identifies an employee amount and “ER” an employer amount, but labels vary by payroll provider.

Compare current-period deductions with the year-to-date totals. An employer contribution displayed for information is not normally another employee deduction. If the stub shows a FICA total and its two components, check whether the total is a summary before adding all three numbers.

FICA vs OASDI: are they the same deduction?

OASDI is the Social Security part of FICA. The letters stand for Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance. FICA also includes Medicare, so a line labeled OASDI does not describe the entire regular FICA amount.

Use the $1,000 pay stub example above: $62 of OASDI plus $14.50 of Medicare equals $76.50 of employee FICA. If the stub also displays a $76.50 FICA total, adding all three figures would count the same tax twice. If it displays an employer total as well, check whether that is an informational amount rather than a deduction.

To reconcile your check, compare the employee deductions with gross pay and net pay, then ask payroll about any unexplained difference. The IRS identifies OASDI and Medicare as the two FICA components.

Why the FICA amount can change during the year

For 2026, Social Security tax applies to covered wages up to $184,500 for the year. Check your year-to-date Social Security wages when a paycheck crosses that limit. Regular Medicare tax continues on covered wages without an annual cap. The SSA publishes the annual Social Security wage base.

An employer begins withholding an extra 0.9% Medicare tax on wages it pays above $200,000 in the calendar year. That payroll trigger is not the same as every taxpayer’s final liability threshold: filing status and other wages can matter. There is no employer match for the extra tax. See IRS Additional Medicare guidance.

Example when a paycheck crosses the Social Security cap

Assume a covered employee has $184,000 in year-to-date Social Security wages from the same employer before a $1,000 paycheck. Only $500 remains below the 2026 cap. Employee Social Security withholding is $500 × 6.2% = $31. If the full $1,000 is Medicare wages, regular Medicare is $14.50. Total employee withholding for these two components is $45.50, assuming no Additional Medicare applies. This is why 7.65% of the whole paycheck would be wrong in this case.

A bonus, wage change, payroll correction or tax-exempt payment can also change the deduction. Ask payroll which taxable wage base it used rather than assuming that every deduction reduces FICA wages.

Employees, employers and self-employed workers

Employees

For many employees, the employer withholds the employee share of Social Security and Medicare taxes from each paycheck and pays an employer share separately. The employer share normally does not appear as an additional deduction from the employee's net pay.

Self-employed workers

Self-employed people generally pay Social Security and Medicare taxes under SECA rather than through employee FICA withholding. That calculation and reporting are different from reading a W-2 paycheck, so this page does not calculate a self-employment tax bill.

How to calculate FICA tax on a paycheck

For a covered employee, calculate the two components separately. In 2026, the employee rate is 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for regular Medicare. The employer generally pays its own matching shares; do not deduct those shares from employee pay.

  1. Identify the wages subject to each tax; gross pay is not always the correct wage base.
  2. Multiply eligible Social Security wages by 6.2%, limiting the taxable wages to the remaining annual wage base.
  3. Multiply Medicare wages by 1.45%; regular Medicare has no annual wage cap.
  4. Add any applicable Additional Medicare withholding, then total the employee amounts.

Example with $1,000 in covered wages

Assume both wage bases are $1,000, the employee is below the annual Social Security cap and no Additional Medicare withholding applies. Social Security is $1,000 × 6.2% = $62. Medicare is $1,000 × 1.45% = $14.50. Total employee FICA is $76.50. Federal income-tax withholding and other deductions are not included.

The combined 7.65% shortcut works only when the same wages are subject to both regular taxes and neither a cap nor Additional Medicare changes the calculation. This is a worked payroll example, not a full take-home-pay estimate. Rates are verified in IRS Topic 751.

What if too much Social Security tax was withheld?

First compare taxable wages, the year’s wage base and the withholding totals. The next step depends on whether one employer made an error or separate employers each withheld tax.

  • One employer’s error: ask that employer to correct the excess. The IRS describes a refund procedure if the employer does not adjust it.
  • Two or more employers: combined Social Security withholding can exceed the annual maximum even when each employer followed its own payroll rules. An excess may qualify for a credit on the tax return.

Do not ask a new employer to stop withholding solely because a previous employer already withheld tax. Special common-paymaster and successor-employer rules can apply. Check IRS Topic 608 on excess Social Security withholding and the applicable return instructions. Medicare does not use the Social Security annual cap.

FICA Tax FAQ

What taxes are included in FICA?

Social Security and Medicare are the two FICA components. Federal income-tax withholding is separate.

Is FICA the same as federal income tax?

No. FICA funds Social Security and Medicare. Federal income-tax withholding is a different paycheck deduction.

Does FICA include Medicare?

Yes. Medicare is one part of FICA, alongside Social Security.

How much FICA is withheld from $1,000 in wages?

With $1,000 subject to both regular taxes, below the Social Security cap and without Additional Medicare withholding, employee FICA is $62 plus $14.50, or $76.50.

Why does my pay stub list Social Security and Medicare separately?

They are separate components of FICA. A displayed FICA total may summarize those two lines rather than represent another deduction.

Do self-employed people pay FICA?

They generally pay Social Security and Medicare taxes under SECA rather than through employee FICA withholding. The employee paycheck example does not calculate self-employment tax.

What does FICA EE mean on my paycheck?

EE usually identifies the employee share of FICA. ER usually identifies the employer share. Payroll labels vary; an employer amount displayed for information is not normally another deduction from your take-home pay.

Is OASDI the same as FICA?

OASDI is the Social Security component of FICA. FICA also includes Medicare. A pay stub may show the components separately or display a combined total.
