How to Calculate a Savings Rate

Calculate a monthly savings rate and understand what the simple percentage leaves out.

The basic method

A simple savings rate divides money saved during a period by gross income for the same period. Definitions of savings and income can vary, so keep periods consistent.

Calculate the percentage step by step

  1. Choose one period, such as a month, for both savings and income.
  2. Record the amount saved under your chosen definition.
  3. Divide that amount by income for the same period, then multiply by 100.
  4. Label whether income is gross or take-home pay before comparing results.

With $600 saved and $5,000 gross income, $600 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 12%. Using $4,000 take-home income instead gives 15%. The savings amount has not changed; only the denominator has.

If income is zero, this division is undefined; track the dollar amount instead. For deciding what to count and interpreting changes over time, read what a savings rate measures.

What should count as savings in your calculation?

Choose a definition before tracking the percentage. One household measure might include new cash savings and employee retirement contributions. Another might track cash savings alone. Label the measure so month-to-month comparisons use the same inputs.

Suppose you add $400 to savings and contribute $200 from your paycheck to retirement during a month with $5,000 gross income. A measure including both contributions is $600 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 12%. A cash-only measure is $400 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 8%.

Moving $1,000 from one existing savings account to another is not $1,000 of new savings. Likewise, a market gain is different from a new contribution. Keep transfers and investment changes separate in your record. For consistent inputs, use the savings rate calculator.

How to calculate an annual savings rate from monthly records

Add the year’s savings and divide by the year’s income under the same definition. Do not simply average monthly percentages when income varies.

For a two-month illustration, saving $300 from $3,000 gives 10% in month one. Saving $1,800 from $6,000 gives 30% in month two. Across both months, the rate is ($300 + $1,800) ÷ ($3,000 + $6,000) × 100 = 23.33%, not the 20% simple average of the two rates.

Use the same method for twelve months. If your measure subtracts withdrawals, record those consistently too. This measures money retained or contributed under your chosen definition, not a retailer’s discount percentage or an investment return.

Savings rate calculation questions

Do I average my monthly savings rates for the year?

When income varies, divide total annual savings by total annual income. A simple average gives small-income and large-income months the same weight.

Can I use take-home pay instead of gross income?

Yes, for a clearly labeled household measure. Use the same definition for every period and avoid subtracting payroll savings twice.
