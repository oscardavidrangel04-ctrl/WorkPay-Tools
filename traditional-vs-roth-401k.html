Traditional vs Roth 401(k): Basic Difference

Compare traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions at a high level and identify the plan-specific details to verify before enrolling.

The short answer

A traditional 401(k) generally uses tax-deferred contributions, with taxes generally due when money is withdrawn. A Roth 401(k) uses after-tax contributions, and qualified withdrawals are generally tax-free. Availability, investment options and employer matching terms depend on the plan.

Compare tax timing and the available plan features

The choice is not determined by one universal rule. Current income, expected future tax circumstances, contribution capacity and plan features can all matter. Tax rules are complex, so use current official materials and consider qualified advice for a personal decision.

Use the right record

Check whether the employer plan offers both options, how matching contributions are handled, what fees apply and whether a vesting schedule applies. Do not confuse the contribution type with the investment selected inside the account.

Official reference

Investor.gov describes traditional and Roth 401(k) options and their broad tax treatment. Read the source.

Separate contribution tax treatment from investments

The traditional or Roth label describes the tax treatment of contributions, not an investment return. Compare the available investments and fees separately.

For a designated Roth account, a qualified distribution generally requires the five-tax-year period plus age 59½, death or disability. A nonqualified distribution can receive different tax treatment. Verify the specific distribution rules rather than treating all Roth withdrawals as tax-free.

For paycheck reporting, see whether 401(k) contributions reduce taxable income.

Source: IRS: designated Roth account FAQs.

Frequently asked questions

Do Roth 401(k) contributions reduce current federal taxable wages?

Generally no. Designated Roth employee contributions are included in current income; traditional pre-tax employee deferrals generally reduce current federal income-tax wages.

Are all Roth 401(k) withdrawals tax-free?

No. Qualified distributions receive tax-free treatment, while nonqualified distributions can have taxable earnings and other consequences. Check the applicable holding-period and distribution requirements.

Can a plan offer both traditional and Roth contributions?

Yes, if the plan provides a designated Roth option. Ask the administrator about available elections and the combined contribution limits.
