Credit report basics

How to Read a Credit Report

A credit report organizes personal details, account history, inquiries and public-record information. Review each section against your own records.

Start with the report details

Confirm the bureau, report date and your personal information. A variation in an address or employer is not automatically a credit error, but an unfamiliar identity detail is worth documenting.

What information appears on a credit report?

Credit reports commonly organize identifying details, credit accounts, payment history, collections and inquiries. The exact information can differ among the three nationwide credit reporting companies because creditors do not necessarily send information to every bureau or at the same time.

Personal information

Review names, current and former addresses, birth date and other identifying details. An old address alone does not necessarily mean identity theft, but an account you do not recognize deserves closer review.

Credit accounts and payment history

Check account ownership, account type, status, balance, credit limit or original loan amount, payment history, and opening or closing dates. Compare these fields with statements, lender notices and your own records. A report may show different update dates across accounts.

Collections and public records

Check whether a collection or public-record entry belongs to you and whether its balance, dates and status match reliable records. If a debt is listed more than once or under a new company name, verify the account details before deciding it is a duplicate.

Credit inquiries

Inquiries show requests to access a credit file. A hard inquiry can follow an application for credit and may be considered by some scoring models. Soft inquiries, such as checking your own report, do not affect scores. For related reporting timelines, see how long credit inquiries stay on a credit report.

Pay attention to the date each account was updated. A balance or status may reflect the furnisher’s last report to the bureau rather than a real-time view. If an account status uses an unfamiliar code, check the report’s legend or ask the bureau or furnisher what that specific code means before treating it as an error.

Check inquiries and unfamiliar entries

Credit reports can list inquiries and accounts reported by lenders or collection agencies. Keep the report copy and supporting records if an item is unfamiliar. Do not assume a similar company name proves it is not yours.

Credit report vs. credit score

A credit report is the underlying history supplied to a reporting company. A credit score is a number calculated from report information using a particular model. Scores can differ because models, bureau data and calculation dates differ. A report correction may or may not change every score. See credit score vs. credit report.

How to get and review your reports

Use AnnualCreditReport.com to request reports from the nationwide bureaus. Review each report separately and save a dated copy for your records. Your own request does not count as a hard credit application.

What to do if you find an error

  1. Mark the exact field that appears inaccurate or incomplete.
  2. Collect supporting documents.
  3. Use the current dispute channel for the bureau reporting the item.
  4. Keep copies of the dispute and response.

FAQ

What should I check first on a credit report?

Start with personal information and account ownership, then compare account status, balances, limits, payment history and dates with your records.

Is a credit report the same as a credit score?

No. A credit report contains information; a score is calculated from report data under a particular scoring model.

Can I dispute an error on a credit report?

You can dispute information you believe is inaccurate or incomplete with each credit reporting company showing it and, when appropriate, with the furnisher. See the step-by-step dispute guide.

Should I remove accurate negative information?

A dispute process is for inaccurate or incomplete information. Accurate negative information is not removed solely because it is unfavorable.
