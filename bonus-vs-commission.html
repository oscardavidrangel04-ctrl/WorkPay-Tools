Bonus vs Commission: How They Differ in Pay

Compare bonus pay and commission pay, see why the terms may affect payroll differently, and review overtime treatment with official guidance.

The short answer

A bonus is compensation paid in addition to regular earnings. A commission is typically pay linked to a sale, revenue or another performance measure set by an agreement. The exact plan document controls eligibility, timing, clawbacks and whether an amount is discretionary.

What to check

Neither label by itself answers whether the amount must be included in a regular-rate calculation for overtime. Under the FLSA, many forms of compensation for hours worked or services rendered are included unless an exclusion applies; the facts and the applicable law matter.

Use the right record

Before accepting or forecasting variable pay, ask what triggers payment, when it is earned versus paid, whether there is a cap, and whether repayment can be required. Use gross estimates for budgeting because withholding can change the net amount.

Official reference

The Department of Labor explains that a bonus is additional compensation and that overtime treatment depends on the type of payment and circumstances. Read the source.

Bonus vs commission: compare the earning trigger

A commission commonly applies a rate or formula to eligible sales. A bonus can depend on a target, a milestone, company results or another condition. Either may be formula-based; calling a payment a “bonus” does not automatically make it discretionary. Compare what earns the payment, not just its label.

Worked example: the same payout from different plans

A 5% commission on $40,000 of eligible sales produces $2,000. A separate plan might award a $2,000 bonus only when a quarterly target is met. The amounts match, but the triggers, timing and effect of a cancelled sale can differ. Use the commission calculator for a flat-rate example and the annual bonus calculator for a salary-percentage target.

Questions to compare before accepting variable pay

Ask when the payment is earned, when it is paid, what sales count and whether returns or cancellations change it. Separate guaranteed base salary from a target that might not be achieved. Review any draw, threshold, cap and repayment clause. The Department of Labor’s bonus guidance explains why overtime treatment depends on the payment’s actual terms, not simply its name.

Frequently asked questions

Can a bonus be nondiscretionary?

Yes. A payment promised under a preset target or formula can be nondiscretionary; the label alone does not settle its treatment.

Can an employee receive salary, bonus and commission?

Yes. A plan can combine several pay components, each with its own earning conditions.

Is commission always a percentage of sales?

No. A plan may use a fixed amount, margin, tiers or other defined measures.

Does equal target pay mean two plans are equivalent?

No. Compare the earning trigger, realistic sales assumptions, timing and adjustment terms as well as the amount.
