What “salary range percentile” means here

This tool calculates a linear position within the two endpoints you enter, then caps the display at 0% and 100%. A $72,000 salary in a $60,000–$90,000 band gives 40%. It does not mean the salary exceeds the earnings of 40% of workers. No salary distribution or market survey is used.

Use uncapped range penetration for outside-band salaries

A salary of $54,000 in that band displays 0% here, while $96,000 displays 100%. The range penetration calculator preserves −20% and 120%, which is more useful for identifying out-of-band pay. The two tools share the underlying linear formula; this page is specifically the bounded display, not a separate market-ranking model.

Calculator FAQ

Is this a real market salary percentile?

No. It is a bounded position between entered endpoints, not a rank within observed salaries.

What does a 50% result mean?

Salary is halfway between the entered minimum and maximum.

Why do outside-range salaries display 0% or 100%?

This tool intentionally caps the output. Use range penetration to see uncapped values.

Can minimum and maximum be equal?

No. A positive range width is required for a meaningful position calculation.

A higher range position can still mean a lower salary

In a $60,000–$90,000 band, $75,000 is at 50% of the range. In an $80,000–$120,000 band, $90,000 is at 25%. The first position is higher within its band even though its dollar salary is lower. A range position cannot rank two different jobs by pay.

Compare like-for-like bands with the same currency, pay period and job context. Use compa-ratio when the question is salary relative to a midpoint, and keep both measures separate from a market percentile based on observed salaries.
