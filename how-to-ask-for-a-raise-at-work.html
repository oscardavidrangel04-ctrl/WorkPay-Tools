How to Ask for a Raise at Work

Build a clear request around your contribution, the role you perform and reliable pay evidence. A good conversation is specific: it explains what changed, what you are requesting and what should happen next.

Reviewed September 13, 2026

Prepare before the meeting

  1. Document results. List completed projects, revenue supported, time saved, quality improvements or responsibilities added.
  2. Check the role. Compare your current work with your job description and note sustained changes in scope.
  3. Research compensation. Use more than one source and match location, experience, company size and responsibilities.
  4. Choose a target. Convert the proposed percentage into annual, monthly, weekly and hourly dollars with the Salary Increase Calculator.
  5. Request a meeting. Ask for dedicated time instead of raising the subject unexpectedly.

A simple raise-request script

Keep the opening short and professional:

“I’d like to discuss my compensation. Over the past [period], I delivered [specific results] and took responsibility for [expanded scope]. Based on that work and the market range for this role, I’d like to discuss adjusting my salary to [target].”

Use your own words. The evidence matters more than memorizing a perfect line. Pause after the request and give the manager time to respond.

How to support the number

Separate evidence into three buckets: business results, increased role scope and relevant market data. Avoid basing the request only on personal expenses. Those expenses may be real, but the strongest workplace case connects compensation to the value and level of the role.

Compare both percentage and dollar changes. A proposed 6% raise on $60,000 is $3,600 annually, $300 monthly and about $69.23 weekly before deductions.

If the answer is not immediate

A manager may need approval or budget information. Ask when you can expect an answer. If the request is declined, ask which measurable outcomes would support reconsideration and agree on a review date. A useful follow-up records the target, evidence requested and timeline without turning the discussion into an argument.

Consider the full package too: base salary, bonus structure, schedule, paid time off, benefits and development opportunities can affect total compensation. Use the Compensation Package Calculator to compare components.

Frequently asked questions

When should I ask for a raise?

After documented results, expanded responsibilities or around a planned performance review can be useful, while recognizing the employer’s budget cycle.

How much should I request?

Use role scope, results, market evidence and internal pay practices. Test the dollar impact with the pay raise calculator.

What if the answer is no?

Ask what measurable goals and timeline would support a future review, then document those next steps.
